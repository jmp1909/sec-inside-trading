Does Insider Buying Predict Returns?

An event study and monthly-rebalanced portfolio backtest of open-market insider purchases and sales across the S&P MidCap 400, built entirely on SEC EDGAR Form 4 structured data and Yahoo Finance daily prices.

01

Executive Summary

Insider buying predicts returns. That's a real finding, not a fluke, but it's a modest tilt rather than a golden signal. Open-market purchases by officers and directors beat an unconditional benchmark at every horizon we tested, from one trading day out to two years, and the effect holds up even after turning it into an actual monthly-rebalanced trading strategy. Here's the catch: the edge only survives risk-adjustment once the portfolio is spread across enough names. Concentrate on the "highest-conviction" bets and the raw returns look spectacular. Once you account for the drawdowns, though, they're worse than just holding the market.

The one-paragraph version

Buy right after an insider purchase and hold for a year, and you'd average +51.3% (median +21.9%) against a +20.8% benchmark. That gap is statistically significant, p < 0.0001. Turn it into an actual strategy, ranking the universe by trailing 3-month net insider dollar buying, holding the top 50, rebalancing monthly, and it beats a diversified buy-and-hold on both return (24.0% vs 19.1% annualized) and risk-adjusted return (Sharpe 0.86 vs 0.83). Go more concentrated, top 10 instead of top 50, and raw returns climb even higher, but the Sharpe ratio drops below the market's. Concentration adds drawdown faster than it adds return.

Methodology

Every data source below got a live test pull before we relied on it. A few candidates didn't survive that check: CoinGecko, Stooq, and OpenSky's historical endpoint were all tried and dropped, and none of them made it into this pipeline.

Universe
S&P MidCap 400, current constituents applied retroactively. That introduces survivorship bias (see Limitations). Pulled from Wikipedia's constituent list and cross-matched to SEC CIKs via company_tickers.json; all 400 tickers resolved.
Insider data
SEC's official quarterly bulk structured datasets (NONDERIV_TRANS.tsv / SUBMISSION.tsv / REPORTINGOWNER.tsv), 33 quarterly files covering 2018Q3 through 2026Q1. Filtered down to open-market codes P (purchase) and S (sale) only. Option exercises, grants, and tax-withholding transactions don't reflect an actual trading decision, so they were left out.
Price data
Yahoo Finance daily adjusted close, full history per ticker, 2,740,075 price-days across 400 tickers.
Event timing
All returns anchored on filing date, not transaction date. This avoids lookahead bias: some filers report years-old transactions late, and the market couldn't have reacted before the filing existed.
Benchmark
Mean forward return from every trading day for every universe ticker, not just insider-filing days (763,483 ticker-day observations at the 1-day horizon). This isolates the insider signal from ordinary mid-cap drift.
Exclusions
3 tickers (CHRD, VAL, WFRD) excluded. A scan of every company's 8-K Item 1.03 filings turned up genuine Chapter 11 reorganizations in the window, and pre/post-reorg price series don't produce continuous, meaningful returns. 181 transactions (0.21% of the dataset) affected.

Reading the mean vs. median gap

At the 2-year horizon, the mean purchase return is +105.5%, but the median is +40.5%. A handful of large winners are pulling the average up. Both numbers are real. The honest one-line summary is "the typical purchase returns about 40% over 2 years, with a fat right tail," not "the average purchase returns 105%."

Portfolio Backtest

The event study shows the signal exists at the individual-transaction level. The harder question: does it survive becoming an actual, tradable, diversified strategy? Here's the setup: rank the universe monthly by trailing net insider dollar value (purchases minus sales), hold the top N equal-weight, rebalance monthly.

We tested whether "many insiders buying" (breadth) or "large value from few buyers" (concentration) refines the base signal, using the best-performing window (3 months). Neither did. The simplest signal won.

05

Key Findings

  1. The signal is real. Insider purchases predict outperformance and sales predict underperformance at every horizon from 1 day to 2 years, against a proper unconditional benchmark, with strong statistical significance (p < 0.02 everywhere, mostly p < 0.0001).
  2. It survives becoming a real strategy. A monthly-rebalanced long portfolio built on trailing net insider dollar value beats a diversified buy-and-hold on raw return in every configuration tested (9 window × size combinations).
  3. But the edge is thin once you price in risk. Only the most diversified configuration (top 50, 3-month window) beats the benchmark's Sharpe ratio. Concentrated top-10 portfolios have eye-catching raw returns and meaningfully worse risk-adjusted performance than just holding the market.
  4. Simple beats clever. Refining the signal with buyer-count (breadth) or value-per-buyer (concentration) underperformed the plain net-dollar-value ranking on every metric we tested. That's a negative result, and it's worth keeping rather than burying.
06

Limitations & Caveats

Survivorship bias

The universe is today's 400 MidCap constituents applied retroactively across all 8 years. Companies that were genuine mid-caps in 2018–2026 but were later acquired, went bankrupt beyond our 3 known cases, or otherwise dropped out of the index are invisible to this study. This likely inflates results somewhat.

Mean vs. median

Headline mean returns (especially at 1–2yr horizons) are pulled upward by a fat right tail of large winners. Medians, reported alongside every mean in this note, are the more representative "typical outcome" figure.

Unofficial price source

Yahoo Finance's chart API is undocumented and could change or rate-limit without notice. It's extremely widely used in practice, but isn't an SLA-backed data source the way SEC EDGAR is.

Data-entry noise

A handful of SEC filings contain evident typos (e.g., a transaction dated 2028, filed 2023). Anchoring all analysis on filing date rather than transaction date renders these harmless, but they're a reminder the underlying filings aren't perfectly clean.
