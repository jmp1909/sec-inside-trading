Does insider buying predict stock returns?

S&P MidCap 400 · SEC Form 4 filings 2018–2026 · portfolio test Feb 2019 – Jul 2026 · latest insider-buying snapshot

Summary

With the corrected method, the insider-buying portfolio did worse than simply holding all index members in equal amounts (8.6% vs 12.2% a year after costs). Most of the first version's apparent edge came from survivorship bias: run on today's index members, as before, the same portfolio returns 24.1% a year before costs and its benchmark 19.1%; on the members at each date they return 10.9% and 12.5%.

  • Measured against the average index member over the same days, stocks moved +0.4% in the 20 trading days after an insider purchase (95% interval −0.4% to +1.2%) and +4.4% over a year (−2.4% to +11.2%). Neither is statistically different from zero. After insider sales the figures were −0.1% and +0.2%.
  • Why the first version's numbers were so much higher, for the one-year return after a purchase: the raw average was 50.4% against 20.6% for an index stock on any day. But the market itself rose 33.7% on average over the same windows, because insiders bought heavily around market lows such as March 2020. That leaves 16.7%. Counting each company and filing day once instead of each transaction row cuts it to 8.1%, and using the index members at each date cuts it to 4.4%.
  • The 50-stock portfolio (3-month look-back, rebalanced monthly) returned 8.6% a year after trading costs of 25 basis points per trade, against 12.2% for the equal-weighted benchmark and 11.8% for the MDY index fund. Its Sharpe ratio was 0.34 (0.50 and 0.50) and its worst drawdown −44% (−37% and −34%).
  • Adjusted for market, size, value, profitability, investment and momentum exposure, its alpha was −2.9% a year (t = −1.4), and −2.1% relative to the equal-weighted benchmark (t = −1.4).
  • Its Sharpe ratio was higher than 83% of 1,000 random 50-stock portfolios from the same stocks (p = 0.17). It beat the equal-weighted benchmark in 2 of 8 calendar years.
  • The first version's one-day return after a purchase (+0.6%) falls to +0.05% relative to the market when trades are entered at the close of the day after the filing rather than the filing day itself. Many Form 4s are filed after the market closes, so most of that first-day move could not have been captured.

Data and method

  • Companies: S&P MidCap 400 members on each date, rebuilt from the current list and Wikipedia's record of index changes. A company only counts while it is in the index. 749 companies were in the index at some point in the period, 349 of which left it before the end. Prices were found for 87% of all company-days in the index (203 of the 349 companies that left). The rest, mostly companies that were taken over and whose old tickers Yahoo no longer carries, are missing.
  • Insider trades: SEC's quarterly Form 3/4/5 data sets from 2018 Q3. Only open-market purchases (code P) and sales (code S). All reporting owners are included: officers, directors and holders of more than 10%.
  • Prices: Yahoo Finance daily closes, adjusted for splits and dividends. If a stock stops trading while held, it is treated as sold at its last price.
  • Timing: returns start at the close of the trading day after the filing date, since many Form 4s are filed after the market closes.
  • Market comparison: each stock's return is compared with the average return of all index members over exactly the same days.
  • Costs and Sharpe ratio: trading costs are charged on the value traded each month. Sharpe ratios use returns above the one-month Treasury bill rate.
  • Bankruptcies: 17 companies filed for Chapter 11 during the period (each filing checked by hand). They are kept up to the filing date, then take a −30% delisting return and leave the universe. The first version dropped such companies entirely.

Limitations

  • Prices are missing for some companies that left the index, mostly takeovers. The −30% bankruptcy delisting return is an average from the literature, not the actual loss for each company. A full fix would need a survivorship-free price database such as CRSP.
  • Wikipedia lists "selected" index changes. The rebuilt membership is checked by requiring about 400 members on every date, but a missed change would not show up.
  • The period (2018–2026) includes the 2020 and 2022 falls but no long bear market. Wikipedia's change history only goes back to 2012, so 2008 can't be added this way.
  • Trading costs are a flat charge per dollar traded, not a market-impact model.
  • The look-back and portfolio size shown in the summary were picked after seeing the first version's results. The full grid and the random-portfolio check are shown for that reason.
  • Yahoo Finance's price API is unofficial.

Changes

The first version (August 2026) used today's index members for the whole period, no trading costs, a Sharpe ratio without a risk-free rate, no factor adjustment, trade-level statistics and same-day entry. All of these were changed in October 2026. The full list, including corrections to the first version's report, is in the changelog.
