Does insider buying predict stock returns?

S&P MidCap 400 · SEC Form 4 filings, Aug 2018 – Mar 2026 · first version, August 2026 · latest insider-buying snapshot

Status, October 2026. The results on this page are from the first version of the analysis. A review found problems that probably make them look better than they are: the stock list has survivorship bias, there are no trading costs, the Sharpe ratio ignores the risk-free rate, there is no control for size or value exposure, and the statistical tests treat related trades as independent. The code has been revised to deal with each of these (see Changes), but the revised version has not been run yet. This page will be replaced with the new results when it has. Until then, treat the numbers below as preliminary.

Summary

  • After insiders bought shares on the open market, the stock on average beat the benchmark (the average S&P 400 stock from an average day) at every horizon from 1 day to 2 years. One year after a purchase the average return was 51.3% (median 21.9%), against 20.8% for the benchmark.
  • After insiders sold, the stock did worse than the benchmark at every horizon.
  • A portfolio of the 50 stocks with the most net insider buying over the past 3 months, rebalanced monthly, returned 24.2% a year from February 2019 to July 2026, against 19.1% for an equal-weighted portfolio of all 400 stocks. Its Sharpe ratio was about the same (0.86 vs 0.83) and its worst drawdown was deeper (−43% vs −37%).
  • Smaller portfolios (10 or 20 stocks) mostly had higher returns but deeper drawdowns. The 10-stock portfolios had clearly lower Sharpe ratios than the 50-stock ones.
  • None of this includes trading costs, and the period was mostly a rising market. See Limitations.

Data and method

  • Companies: the 400 members of the S&P MidCap 400 in August 2026 (list from Wikipedia), matched to SEC company numbers (CIKs). The same list was used for the whole period.
  • Insider trades: SEC's quarterly Form 3/4/5 data sets, 2018 Q3 to 2026 Q1. Only open-market purchases (code P) and sales (code S); grants, option exercises and tax withholding are left out. All reporting owners are included: officers, directors and holders of more than 10%.
  • Prices: Yahoo Finance daily closing prices, adjusted for splits and dividends.
  • Timing: returns start from the close on the filing date, not the trade date, because the filing is when the trade becomes public.
  • Benchmark for the event study: the average forward return, at the same horizon, of every stock in the list starting from every trading day in the period.
  • Excluded: three companies that went through Chapter 11 during the period (CHRD, VAL, WFRD), because their price histories are not continuous across the reorganization.

Sample: 6,443 purchase rows and 81,085 sale rows at the short horizons, falling to 5,338 and 63,650 at two years (later trades don't have two years of prices yet). The counts are individual transaction rows. One filing often reports several lots, and several insiders at the same company often buy on the same day, so the number of independent events is smaller than shown and these p-values are too optimistic. Means at the longer horizons are pulled up by a few very large winners; the medians are a better guide to a typical outcome (for example, 41% rather than 105% over two years).

Limitations

These are the main reasons to doubt the numbers above, and what the revised code does about each.

  • Survivorship bias. The stock list is today's index members applied to all eight years. It leaves out companies that were in the index but were later acquired, delisted or dropped, and it includes companies that only joined after strong gains. Revision: index membership is rebuilt for each date from the history of index changes, and removed companies are kept.
  • No trading costs; Sharpe without a risk-free rate. The top-50 portfolio replaces a large part of its holdings every month. Revision: turnover is measured and costs of 0, 10, 25 and 50 basis points per trade are applied; the Sharpe ratio uses returns above the Treasury bill rate.
  • Deeper drawdowns than the benchmark. Even the best combination fell 43% at its worst, against 37% for the benchmark. Revision: the strategy is also compared with the MDY index fund, at the benchmark's level of volatility, and hedged against the market.
  • No adjustment for factor exposure. The extra return could come from holding smaller or cheaper stocks rather than from insider information. Revision: returns are regressed on the Fama-French five factors plus momentum.
  • Few purchases. There are about 6,443 purchase rows against 81,085 sale rows, and many of the purchase rows belong to the same event. Revision: trades are grouped into one event per company and filing day, standard errors allow for events clustering by company and by month, and portfolios that hold every recent buyer are tested month by month.
  • Mostly a rising market. The period had two sharp falls (2020 and 2022) but no long bear market. Revision: each event is compared with the market over the same dates, and results are shown by year and for rising and falling months separately. The period cannot easily be extended to cover 2008, because the index-change history used for the stock list only goes back to 2012.
  • Same-day entry. Many Form 4s are filed after the market closes, so buying at that day's close is not always possible. Revision: the event study enters at the next day's close.

Changes

October 2026

Method (code changed, not yet run): point-in-time index membership; trading costs and turnover; Sharpe ratio above the T-bill rate; MDY index fund as a second benchmark; factor regressions; event-level statistics with clustered standard errors and market-adjusted returns; next-day entry; random-portfolio and calendar-time checks; results by year and market direction. Details are in the changelog.

Corrections to this page:

  • The benchmark was described as "buy-and-hold". It is an equal-weighted portfolio of all stocks, rebalanced monthly.
  • The page said the purchases were made by "officers and directors". The data also include holders of more than 10%.
  • The page said the alternative rankings did worse "on every metric". They had lower returns and Sharpe ratios but slightly smaller drawdowns.
  • The grid of all combinations showed numbers from a different run than the saved results file (data/portfolio_backtest_grid.csv), differing by up to 0.9 percentage points a year. The grid and summary now use the saved file. The chart and the alternative-ranking table come from the other run (24.0% a year instead of 24.2% for the main combination). Which run is correct can't be checked without re-running; the revised code produces all of these from a single run.
  • Points on the chart were labelled with the first day of each month although they show the value at the end of the month. They are now plotted at month end.
  • Simpler wording and design.
